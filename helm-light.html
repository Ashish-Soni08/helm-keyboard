Concept and build brief, prepared for NuPhy · 19 November 2026

Keep the verbs, drop the alphabet.

Helm is a keyboard for a desk where you talk to your computer and software agents do the work. Speech runs at about 150 words a minute and typing at about 40, so the sentences move to your voice. Six keys stay. None of them was ever a letter.

This page asks NuPhy to judge whether Helm can be built and, if so, to build a first working prototype. Helm is a design concept. No CAD, circuit board, firmware or prototype exists, and every size below comes from the model.

Why these six

Voice is the fast channel and the loose one. Hands are slow and exact. The board gives each the work it is good at. It also lets an agent ask for attention by moving something at the edge of your sight.

The floorwas Space
A voice has no edge. The machine cannot tell talking to it from talking near it, so holding the bar is the edge. The microphone is a stalk that sits below the deck unless the bar is down. You can see when it is listening.
The yeswas Enter
Consent has to be deliberate, and nobody should be able to give it from across the room. So it stays a key, and its stiffness is set by what is at stake. Filing a draft is a 1.6 N tap. Deleting a year of backups is a slow push through 5.7 N, more than ten ordinary keys at once.
The take-backwas Backspace
Every act an agent completes turns the undo wheel one notch. Turning it back is the same gesture whether you are refusing a request or reversing something already done.
The leashwas Arrows
Four stops: watch, ask, act, run. The lever sets how much an agent may do before it has to raise its key. There is no fifth stop. Nothing irreversible goes through without a hand.
The haltwas Escape
A mushroom button you can hit without looking. It latches, and every agent holds still until you release it.
The letterswas Caps Lock
Names, passwords and symbols still need spelling, so the alphabet is kept, in a drawer. The least useful key on the old board finally has a job. It lets the letters out.

Build path

Six stages. NuPhy can stop after any of them, and the first needs no new hardware. No stage has a duration here, because those are NuPhy’s to estimate.

  1. Prove the interaction on a board you already sell. Take a Kick75 with QMK firmware. Remap six keys to talk, yes, take back, leash, halt and letters. Write a small desktop app that listens while Space is held, hands the sentence to an assistant, and waits for Enter before anything risky runs.Done when five people can run a morning’s tasks by voice and say whether the six keys feel right.
  2. Add stakes without a motor. Move the yes key to a magnetic switch. Let the app send a stakes value from 0 to 1, and have the firmware require a deeper and longer press as it rises, up to the full 3.3 mm held for about a second.Done when testers can tell a low-stakes request from a high-stakes one by feel alone.
  3. Build the fixed parts as a bench prototype. A printed enclosure at the model’s 300 × 108 mm footprint with the talk bar, five agent keys with lamps, the ribbon display, the undo wheel, the leash lever and the latching halt button. The microphone is fixed, but wired so that its supply line is cut unless the bar is down.Done when the whole tour in the model can be played on real hardware, minus the four moving parts.
  4. Develop the four moving parts, each on its own test rig.
    • Consent key: motor-wound spring preload. Measure force accuracy, noise, the time to wind from 0.5 N to 6 N, and cycle life.
    • Agent keys: a lifter that raises a cap 6 mm and still lets it be pressed.
    • Microphone stalk: a 14 mm rise that survives being pushed down by hand.
    • Letter drawer: rails, flex cable and a key technology thin enough for the tray.
    Done when each part has a measured cost, noise level and failure rate.
  5. Decide what earns its place, then build an integrated sample. Test the moving parts against their fixed stand-ins from stages 2 and 3 with real users. Keep only the ones that measurably help. Then make one custom board and aluminium case with what is left.Done when there is one sample that works end to end and a list of what was cut and why.
  6. Design for manufacture. Tooling, radio and battery certification, drop and dust tests for the moving parts, price.Done when NuPhy can quote a unit cost and a launch date.

Firmware and host software

Helm is a normal USB keyboard and microphone plus one extra channel of small messages between the board and a companion app. The app talks to whichever assistant the owner uses. The board never needs to know which.

Three rules the firmware should hold to:

  • Only the key can say yes. The app can raise the stakes but can never send a Yes itself.
  • The microphone is off in hardware. Its supply is switched by the talk bar, not by software.
  • Halt wins. While the button is latched the board sends no Yes, whatever else is pressed.

In the model the app scores stakes from the verb of the instruction: 0.05 for find, 0.2 for draft, 0.45 for send, 0.62 for pay or book, 0.95 for delete. The leash stops ask at 0, 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8. A real product would need a better scorer than a verb list. The scale is the part worth keeping.

Open questions and risks

The model was drawn to explain an idea, and several of its sizes would not survive engineering as they stand.

  • Does force do more than delay? Nobody has tested whether a stiffer key reduces wrong approvals better than a longer hold. Stages 2 and 5 exist to answer this.
  • The letter keys are too small and the drawer too shallow. A 9.6 mm pitch is half a normal key, and the tray is 10 mm tall, which no mechanical switch fits. It needs a thin key technology or a deeper deck.
  • The consent key stands 33 mm proud. That is easy to see and hard to carry. A lower mechanism, or one that winds a spring sideways under the deck, may be needed.
  • Motors in a keyboard. Four moving parts add cost, noise, dust paths and things to break. They may also rule out long battery life.
  • Who makes the software? The board is only half a product. It needs a companion app and an agreed message format, from NuPhy, a partner, or an open specification.
  • People who cannot speak or cannot press 6 N. The letters cover the first. The second needs a user-set ceiling on the force.
  • Privacy of speech. Whether recognition runs on the computer or in a service is the app’s choice, but the board should make listening visible either way.
  • No prior-art or patent search has been done.
